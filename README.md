# Narrative Lens

A way of hearing what a group of people actually experienced, rather than what
a survey guessed they would say.

People tell you a short story about something that really happened. Then — and
this is the part that matters — **they say what their own story means**, by
placing a marker between three pulls, sliding it along a line, or choosing an
answer. Nobody interprets on their behalf. When two hundred people have each
done that, the pattern in where they put their markers is a picture of the
place they work, and it is a picture nobody wrote.

This app runs on your own computer. Your stories live in a file on that
computer, not on anybody's service.

---

## Starting and stopping

| What you want | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Start the app | Double-click **Start Narrative Lens.bat**. Your browser opens by itself. |
| Stop the app | Close the small window called *Narrative Lens server*. |
| First time on a new computer | Double-click **Set up Narrative Lens.bat**, once. It needs the internet and takes a few minutes. After that it is only ever the double-click. |

If the app will not start, it tells you what went wrong and what to do about
it, in a sentence. That is deliberate: there is nothing in this app you are
expected to fix by editing a file.

## The four tabs, in the order you will use them

### 1. Studio — write the questions

This is where everything a respondent sees is written: the story prompt, the
three corners of each triangle, the ends of each slider, the words on every
option. A phone-shaped preview beside it shows exactly what they will see, and
an estimate of how long it will take them.

**One thing to know.** Once stories exist, changing the words is not free. The
app will ask you which kind of change you are making:

- **Fix wording** — a typo, or the same question said more clearly. Applied in
  place, and written into that version's edit log.
- **Change meaning** — you are asking something different now. This starts a
  new version. Stories already told stay attached to the words those people
  actually saw, which is the only honest way to keep them.

The app asks because only you know which one it is.

### 2. Capture & Links — collect the stories

Five ways in, and every one of them stamps how it arrived:

- **One at a time** — you type a story in yourself.
- **From paper** — rapid entry for a stack of returned paper forms.
- **Links & QR** — open a link, print its QR poster, put it where people work.
  Phones reach it over your Tailscale network. Close the link when you are done
  and it stays closed.
- **Kiosk** — a machine left out at a workshop, resetting itself after every
  story.
- **Import & Validate** — a file of stories you already have (next tab).

### 3. Import & Validate — bring in stories you already have

Point it at a Word file, a PDF, a slide deck, a spreadsheet, a CSV, or a
meeting transcript. It happens in two halves, and **you stand between them**:

1. **Organise.** The app reads the file and proposes where one story stops and
   the next begins — or, for a spreadsheet, which column holds the story. You
   check it and confirm. Nothing goes on until you do.
2. **Mark up.** Only then does it suggest where each story sits on your
   questions. Every suggestion waits for you in **Waiting for you**, where you
   accept it, move the markers yourself, or reject the story.

Nothing the AI proposes ever enters your data on its own — not at high
confidence, not at low. A suggestion the app is unsure about is tinted amber so
you can spend your attention where it is worth spending, but it queues exactly
like every other one.

### 4. Patterns — read what you have

Opens on the **Narrative Landscape**. There is a one-pager on reading it below.
Three more ways of looking sit beside it: the quieter **supporting charts** (who
told the stories, how they answered the sliders and choices), the **3D
Explorer**, and the **story browser** — the stories themselves, searchable, with
a star for the ones worth coming back to and tags in your own words. When a hill
in the landscape surprises you, that is where you go next.

Four things to download:

- **The stories (CSV)** — every story with its full history: how it arrived,
  when, which version of the questions it answered, who moved which marker.
- **The Pattern Brief** — a short written summary. Every sentence in it is
  arithmetic you could redo by hand; no AI writes a word of it.
- **What we heard** — the version to give back to the people who told the
  stories. No story text, nothing about how a story arrived, and nothing that
  fewer than five people said.
- **A picture** — the landscape as a contour, or the supporting charts as one
  sheet. Both save in black on white, ready to paste into a document or put
  through a photocopier.

And from the story browser, a CSV of just the stories you ticked.

## One-pager: printing a paper pack

Sometimes the room has no phones, or the point of the session is the sticky
dots on the wall. A paper pack lets you run the whole thing on paper and type
it up afterwards.

**To print one**

1. Go to the **Studio** and choose your question set.
2. Click **Paper pack for version 1** (the number is whichever version you are
   on). A print-ready page opens.
3. Your browser's print dialog appears. Choose your printer — or choose *Save
   as PDF* if you want to send it somewhere to be printed.
4. Print it single-sided. Every sheet is designed to be photocopied in plain
   black and white, so a copier will not lose anything.

**What comes out**

- **The story card**, one per respondent. It carries the prompt, room to write,
  and the anonymity statement word for word as it appears on screen.
- **One large sheet per question** — a triangle, a line, or a square, printed
  big enough for a sticky dot. These go on the wall.
- **The facilitator sheet**, with a grid for reconciling what came back: how
  many cards you handed out, how many returned, how many you have typed in.

**Afterwards**

Go to **Capture & Links → From paper** and type them in. That mode is built for
a stack: the fields are in the order they appear on the card, and each entry is
stamped as having come from paper, so you can always tell the paper stories
from the typed ones later.

**Two things worth doing**

- Write the version number on the top of the pile. If you change the questions
  later, you will want to know which wording those people answered.
- Count the cards before and after. The facilitator sheet has a place for it,
  and a count that does not match is the kind of thing that is impossible to
  reconstruct a week later.

## One-pager: reading a landscape

**What you are looking at.** Every story that answered one triangle is a point
inside it — nearer the corner it leaned towards. The landscape is how thickly
those points lie. A hill is a place where many stories landed close together.
That is all it is.

**Height is thickness, not importance.** A tall peak means "a lot of stories
sit here", never "this matters more". Some of the most important things in a
set of stories are a lonely point in a corner.

**Read the peaks, not the slopes.** The slopes between hills are smoothing —
the arithmetic filling in the space between the points it was given. The peaks
are the finding. Each is labelled with what it holds ("4 near Speed"), and
clicking one shows you exactly those stories, not roughly those stories.

**Use the contour when you want to measure.** The **Contour** button gives you
the same landscape seen from directly overhead, drawn as rings, with every
story as a dot. It is easier to read precisely, it prints, and it is what a
saved picture gives you.

**Triangles pull against themselves.** Each answer is three weights that must
add to one, so a story cannot move towards one corner without moving away from
the others. A rise on one corner is a fall on another — that is arithmetic, not
a finding. Read the shape, not the cause.

**Compare with the side-by-side.** Splitting the landscape by group, or by how
the stories arrived, gives you a panel each, all drawn to one shared height, so
comparing them by eye is honest.

**What a landscape cannot tell you.** It says where stories sit. It never says
why they sit there. When a hill surprises you, the next move is to click it and
read the stories under it — that is what the picture is for.

**A statistical cluster is a place to look, not a cause.** The 3D Explorer can
draw clusters over the points. They carry the label *statistical clusters —
descriptive only*, and they mean it: a cluster says these answers are near each
other, and nothing else.

## What the app will never do

- **Interpret a story for anybody.** The person who lived it says what it
  means. The AI's only jobs are finding where one story stops and the next
  begins, and proposing marker positions for you to check.
- **Put an AI suggestion into your data without you.** Every one queues for a
  human decision, at every confidence level.
- **Write a pattern.** Every figure, hill, ring and headline is counted by
  ordinary arithmetic on your own machine. No AI generates, smooths, labels or
  narrates any pattern.
- **Record who anybody is.** There is no name, no email, no device, no network
  address anywhere — not hidden, not encrypted: not collected. Times are
  rounded to the hour so a story cannot be matched to who was on shift. The
  anonymity statement on the story card is a description of the code.
- **Go online without being asked.** Capture, validation, every pattern, every
  export and all printing work with the network unplugged. The only thing that
  reaches out is the **Analyse** button, and only when you click it.

## Keeping your data

Everything lives in the `data` folder next to the app — one file. Copy that
folder and you have copied your stories; put it back and they are back. If
somebody sets this up on a new computer for you, that folder is the only thing
that has to travel.

## For whoever maintains this

Run `./run_checks.sh`. It is green or it is not: linting, the test suite, the
frontend build, and an end-to-end smoke test. `PROGRESS.md` holds the phase
plan and the decisions; `LATEST.md` holds the current state and how to resume.
`NL_MOCK_AI=1` runs the whole app, including both AI stages, with no network at
all.
